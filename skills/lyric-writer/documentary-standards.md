# Documentary & True Crime Legal Standards

Guidelines for writing lyrics about real people and events.

These are the plugin's conservative house rules for staying close to the record. They are not legal advice, and following them does not make a lyric safe to publish: what counts as defamation or an invasion of privacy depends on the country, on whether the person is alive and on the facts. For a release about a living person or an open case, have someone qualified read it.

---

## When to Apply These Standards

Apply documentary legal standards for:
- **True crime albums** (criminal cases, trials, investigations)
- **Documentary storytelling** (real events, real people)
- **Biographical work** (artist profiles of real people)
- **Historical narratives** (factual events from history)
- **Any work citing sources** (if RESEARCH.md or SOURCES.md exists)

---

## Core Legal Principles

**The Five Rules** (the same five as in the lyric-writer skill; when the wording differs, the meaning is the same):
1. **No impersonation** - Use third-person narrator voice only, never speak AS the person. This one is a house style choice more than a legal line: it keeps the song from putting invented speech in a real mouth.
2. **No fabricated quotes** - Words in quotation marks are verbatim from a source, and the source is recorded in the track file
3. **No internal state claims without a source** - A thought, feeling, belief or motive goes in only when testimony, an interview or a record states it, and the line attributes it ("she testified she was afraid"). Unsourced, it stays out
4. **No speculative actions** - Stick to documented events, don't invent daily activities or scenes
5. **No factual claims the sources don't make** - That includes negatives: "nobody saw" needs a source that says so, because a gap in the record is not evidence. It also includes guilt: someone charged or sued is "charged with" or "accused of" until a verdict or judgment says otherwise

## What to AVOID (High Risk)

### Internal State Claims
Unsourced, each of these is the narrator claiming to know a mind. With a source, attribute it (see Attribution Techniques).
- ❌ "She was afraid"
- ❌ "He felt angry"
- ❌ "She believed him"
- ❌ "He knew it was wrong"
- ❌ "She wanted to escape"
- ❌ "Living in fear"
- ❌ "Tried to disappear"
- ❌ "Can't believe what's real"
- ❌ "Didn't know where to go"

### Fabricated Quotes
- ❌ "I need help," she cried
- ❌ "We'll start the process," they said
- ❌ Direct speech without documented source

### Speculative Actions
- ❌ "She finally made the call" (unless documented HOW)
- ❌ "Goes to sleep that night" (undocumented private action)
- ❌ "Waiting for the call" (speculative daily activity)
- ❌ "June 21st she waited" (inventing specific undocumented timeline)

### Negative Factual Claims
- ❌ "Nobody heard her scream"
- ❌ "Nobody saw what happened"
- ❌ "No one knew the truth"

Rewording does not fix these. "The walls kept her screams in" still asserts that she screamed and that nobody heard; if the record does not say so, the line is the same claim in a metaphor.

### Guilt Before a Verdict
- ❌ "He killed her" (charged, not convicted)
- ❌ "The company stole the money" (alleged in a lawsuit)
- ❌ "Years of abuse" (stated as fact when only one side has alleged it)

## What to USE Instead (Closer to the Record)

### Pattern-Based Language
Use a pattern when the sources document a pattern but not its individual incidents. A pattern is still a factual claim: it needs a source as much as a single event does, and a vague claim is not a safer one.
- ✅ "The court found a pattern of abuse" (the finding, attributed)
- ✅ "Four police reports in three years" (what the record counts)
- ❌ "The cycle repeated" with nothing in the sources about a second incident

### Observable Behavior
- ✅ "She called 911" (documented action)
- ✅ "Court records show" (attributed to source)
- ✅ "Testimony revealed" (documented in trial)
- ✅ "The neighbors told police they heard nothing" (a negative, but a sourced and attributed one)

### Documented Psychiatric/Medical States
- ✅ "Brief reactive psychosis" (diagnosis from doctor)
- ✅ "PTSD confirmed" (documented diagnosis)
- ✅ "Testimony says she lost her mind" (attributed to testimony)

### Narrator Description vs. Internal Claims
- ✅ "She asked for protection" (observable, documented)
- ❌ "She felt desperate" (internal state)
- ✅ "She filed for a protective order" (what she did)
- ❌ "Reality hitting hard" / "She couldn't process" (both describe her mind, however they are phrased)
- ✅ "Four years of violence, the court found" (documented and attributed)
- ❌ "Four years of hiding" (internal intention)

### Timing Without Speculation
- ✅ "Two days before June 23rd" (documented timing)
- ❌ "June 21st she waited, June 22nd still nothing" (speculative daily actions)

## Attribution Techniques

When you need to describe something close to internal state, **attribute it to a source:**

- ✅ "Doctors diagnosed PTSD"
- ✅ "Testimony revealed years of abuse"
- ✅ "Court records documented the pattern"
- ✅ "She testified 'You hurt me again and again'" (actual quote with attribution)
- ✅ "According to trial testimony" (explicit attribution)
- ✅ "Prosecutors said he planned it for weeks" (an accusation, given as an accusation)
- ✅ "The jury found him guilty in March" (a verdict, given as a verdict)

Attribution is what separates the narrator reporting a claim from the narrator making it. Use it for internal states, for negatives and for anything not yet decided by a court.

## Before/After Examples

### Example 1: Internal State
- ❌ BEFORE: "She was living in dread, trying to survive"
- ✅ AFTER: "She told the court she lived in fear for years" (if she did; otherwise the line goes)

### Example 2: Fabricated Quote
- ❌ BEFORE: "'I need protection,' she begged"
- ✅ AFTER: "She asked for protection, asked to get away"

### Example 3: Speculative Action
- ❌ BEFORE: "She finally made the call, hiding in the bathroom"
- ✅ AFTER: "She called 911 at 9:40 that night" (what the call log shows; the bathroom is invented)

### Example 4: Negative Claim
- ❌ BEFORE: "Nobody heard her scream, nobody saw"
- ✅ AFTER: "Two neighbors testified they heard nothing that night" (if they did; otherwise say only what the record shows happened)

### Example 5: Pattern vs. Specific
- ❌ BEFORE: "The first time he hit her, the second time worse"
- ✅ AFTER: "Three reports to police, each one worse than the last" (if the reports say so)

### Example 6: Accused vs. Convicted
- ❌ BEFORE: "He poisoned her coffee, watched her fade"
- ✅ AFTER: "Prosecutors say he poisoned her coffee; the trial starts in May"

## The Documentary Standard

**Ask yourself for EVERY line:**
1. Can I cite this claim to a specific source?
2. Am I claiming internal knowledge I can't have?
3. Am I inventing quotes or dialogue?
4. Am I speculating about undocumented actions?
5. Am I making negative claims I can't prove?
6. Am I stating as fact something that is still only alleged?

**If the answer to ANY of these is problematic, rewrite the line.**

---

## Why This Matters

Legal issues can:
- Expose you to defamation claims
- Undermine the credibility of the entire work
- Require expensive revisions or takedowns
- Damage your reputation as a documentary artist

**The standard**: If you can't cite it, don't write it. If you can't prove it, don't claim it. If it's internal, don't assume it.
